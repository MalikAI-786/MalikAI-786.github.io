# The study design

The qualitative arm of doctoral research on what happens to professional
judgment when the analysis arrives already formed.

The dissertation itself stays quantitative. This design exists to do something
the survey cannot: find out *how* the judgment is actually made, in the
practitioner's own words, and turn that into propositions that can be tested
against the constructs already in the instrument.

---

## The research question

> **How do internal auditors describe forming and revising a professional
> judgment after reviewing an initial LLM-generated assessment?**

It opens with *how*. It is open-ended. It is non-directional — it does not
presume that the judgment is distorted, only that it is formed somehow. It
names who. It ends in a question mark.

### Why it is not phrased around bias

The obvious version — *"How does anchoring bias affect auditor judgment when
using LLMs?"* — fails twice over. It presumes the bias operates, which is
directional, and it presumes an effect, which is the grammar of a hypothesis
rather than a question. Anchoring is a construct imported from the quantitative
side. Here the unit is the **process**: what the auditor does between seeing the
machine's answer and committing to their own.

Keep *factors*, *variables*, *impact*, *effect* and *relationship* out of the
question entirely. They carry measurement assumptions that this phase has not
earned yet.

---

## Approach: phenomenology, analysed with grounded-theory technique

**Phenomenology**, because the unit of analysis is the lived experience of
forming a judgment, and because it needs no site. There is no organisation to
negotiate access with, no gatekeeper to satisfy, no bounded case to define.
Participants are simply people who have lived the phenomenon. That removes the
single largest practical barrier to running this at all.

**Grounded-theory analysis**, because phenomenology alone tends to stop at rich
description. Grounded theory ends in propositions, and propositions are what
bridge back to a testable instrument. The two combine cleanly: the approach
governs sampling and collection, the analytic technique governs what happens to
the transcripts.

Case study is the viable alternative if a single audit function ever grants
access — multiple cases would allow comparison, which is where case study earns
its keep. It is not the first choice here because access is the constraint.

---

## Sampling

**Purposive, criterion-based.** Not random, and not aiming at
representativeness — that is the quantitative phase's job.

Inclusion criteria:

- Practising internal auditor or audit manager
- Has used an LLM-assisted tool on live engagement work, not only in training
- Within a defined recency window, so the recollection is of practice rather
  than of an impression

Recruit for **variation** across seniority, sector and tooling maturity, on the
principle that a phenomenon shows its shape at its edges. Sample to saturation
rather than to a number, and record in the decision log the point at which
successive interviews stopped producing new codes.

---

## Data collection — three sources, deliberately

Triangulation is not three methods for their own sake. Each source is here
because it catches something the others miss.

| Source | What it catches | What it misses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Semi-structured interview | The account the auditor gives of their own reasoning | Reconstruction after the fact; the reasoning they cannot articulate |
| Think-aloud on a seeded task | Reasoning as it happens, including the pauses | Performance under observation — the Hawthorne problem |
| Document review — workpapers, review notes | What was actually recorded and signed off | Nothing about what was considered and discarded |

The interview supplies the narrative, the think-aloud supplies the behaviour,
and the documents supply the record. Where the three disagree, the disagreement
is the finding.

**Collection and analysis run in parallel.** This is not a shortcut — it is the
method. Early coding shapes later interviews, and constant comparison is only
possible if there is already something to compare against.

---

## Analysis

**First-order and second-order, held apart.** First-order stays in the
participant's own words and terms. Second-order is the researcher's theoretical
reading. Reporting them separately lets a reader see exactly where the informant
stops and the author begins — which is the whole basis on which a qualitative
claim can be audited.

**Constant comparison.** Each new transcript is compared against what has
already been found, not coded in isolation. Codes that survive contact with new
data earn their place; codes that do not get recorded as having failed, in the
decision log, at the time.

**Propositions, not hypotheses.** The output is a set of statements that could
be tested, phrased as propositions because nothing here has been tested yet.

---

## Validation

| Technique | What it defends against |
| --- | --- |
| Member checking | The researcher's reading drifting from what the participant meant |
| Insider / outsider pairing | Immersion turning into identification |
| Audit trail | A reasoning chain reconstructed after the conclusion |
| Reflexivity journal | Unexamined position shaping what gets asked and heard |
| Triangulation | Any single source being taken at face value |

Findings are **transferable within a comparable context**, not generalisable.
That is a limit of the approach, not a defect in the execution, and it should be
stated plainly rather than hedged.

---

## The reflexivity instrument

This is the part of the design that is not standard, and it is the part most
worth defending.

The existing dashboards on this account already do one thing repeatedly:
**predict, record the prediction before the outcome is known, then score the
prediction against what actually happened.** `mizan/` does it with
forecast-versus-actual calibration charts. The SPX work does it with a
calibration overlay drawn from a rolling ledger of morning calls against
end-of-day outcomes.

That is structurally identical to what a reflexivity journal is supposed to
achieve, and it is more rigorous than the usual practice of writing impressions
into a notebook.

So the procedure is:

1. **Before each interview**, record the themes expected to appear. Timestamped,
   committed, not editable afterwards.
2. **After coding that transcript**, score the prediction against the codes that
   actually emerged.
3. **Track the calibration over the series.** A researcher whose predictions
   keep coming true is either unusually well attuned or is hearing what they
   came to hear. Both are findings. Only one of them is good.

The output is a quantified researcher-bias trace that can be reported alongside
the analysis instead of asserted in a paragraph.

**Why this is not a gimmick here.** The subject of this research is over-reliance
on a system that agrees with you. A method that cannot detect the researcher
agreeing with himself would be making the same error it is studying. The
instrument has to hold for the person holding it.

---

## Bridge back to the dissertation

The reason this is worth doing rather than only submitting:

```
propositions  ->  candidate survey items  ->  mapped to existing constructs
```

Each proposition that survives analysis becomes a candidate item. Items map onto
constructs already in `instrument/`. The result is instrument-validation
evidence for a quantitative dissertation — obtained without changing the
dissertation's methodology, its structure, or its IRB classification.

The coursework deliverable and the dissertation-strengthening work become one
artifact rather than two.

---

## What this design cannot do

- **It cannot generalise.** Nothing here supports a population claim.
- **It cannot establish that anchoring occurs.** It can describe a process in
  which something anchoring-shaped is reported. Establishing it is the survey's
  job.
- **It cannot escape the Hawthorne problem.** Being watched changes the work.
  Triangulation offsets it; nothing removes it.
- **It cannot be run under the existing IRB approval.** Collecting interviews is
  human-subjects research and needs its own protocol or an amendment. Writing
  the proposal needs nothing. Running it needs approval.
