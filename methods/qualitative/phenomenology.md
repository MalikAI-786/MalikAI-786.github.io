# Phenomenology

## What it is
The study of a phenomenon as it is lived, across the people who have lived it.
The unit of analysis is the experience itself, not a place or an organisation.
Participants qualify by having been through the thing, wherever they were.

## When it applies
When the question is *what is this like* or *how is this experienced*, and when
the phenomenon crosses organisational boundaries rather than sitting inside one.
It is the practical choice when there is no site to gain access to — no company
to negotiate with, no gatekeeper, no bounded case. That single property makes it
the most runnable of the five approaches for a working researcher.

## What it cannot do
It cannot describe a process embedded in one organisation's culture — that is
ethnography's work. It cannot compare across cases, because there are no cases.
It produces rich description and tends to stop there; if propositions are
wanted, an analytic technique has to be brought in on top of it. And it is
entirely dependent on what participants can articulate, which excludes the
reasoning they perform without being able to narrate.

## Citation
Moustakas (1994); Creswell & Poth.

## How I explain this
"I'm not studying a company. I'm studying a thing that happens to people, and
I go and find the people it happened to. The phenomenon is the site."
