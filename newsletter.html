Proof Over Promise

Every vendor deck says the model is governed. Almost none of them show you the evidence. This is a newsletter about the difference, written by someone who used to sit on the examiner's side of that table.

Subscribe Read the about ↗

What it is

AI governance has developed a vocabulary problem. Organizations describe controls they intend to build, frameworks they have mapped to, and oversight they plan to exercise. Very little of it is written down in a form anyone could examine.

I spent twenty years on the other end of that gap. As a bank examiner, then running audits inside global banks, the recurring finding was rarely that a control was missing. It was that the control existed on paper and nobody could produce evidence it had ever operated.

Proof Over Promise is about what that evidence actually looks like for AI systems: model inventories that are current rather than aspirational, human oversight that is designed rather than assumed, and documentation that survives contact with somebody who is allowed to disagree with you.

What it covers

Four recurring threads, drawn from doctoral research and from work already shipped inside regulated institutions.

Thread 01 · Governance

Frameworks, and what they leave to you

NIST AI RMF, SR 26-2, ISO/IEC 42001, the EU AI Act. What each actually requires, where they overlap, and the decisions they hand back to the practitioner without admitting it.

Thread 02 · Judgment

What happens when the analysis arrives already formed

My doctoral research, in plain language. Automation bias, over-reliance, and the uncomfortable finding that systems are most agreeable exactly where a reviewer most needs to be contradicted.

Thread 03 · Evidence

The documentation an examiner will ask for

Written from the examiner's seat. What gets asked, in what order, and why the answer "we have a policy" ends the conversation badly.

Thread 04 · Practice

Things I built, including what broke

Shipping AI into a live audit function, with the parts that did not work. A record that only contains successes is not a record.

Who it is for

  • Internal audit, risk, and compliance leaders being asked to assure AI they did not build
  • Model risk teams extending SR 26-2 practice to systems that do not behave like models
  • Anyone preparing to explain their AI controls to a regulator
  • Researchers working near judgment, automation bias, and professional skepticism
On how this is written. I use AI tools intensively, which is precisely why I study their failure modes. Drafting assistance is used and disclosed. The judgment, the argument, and any claim about what happened in a real institution are mine, and I would rather be corrected than agreed with. If something here is wrong, tell me.
