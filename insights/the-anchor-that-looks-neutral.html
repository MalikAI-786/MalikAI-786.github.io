Why does the most neutral-looking document
move a reviewer the most?

A reviewer is moved most by a conclusion that does not look like it is trying to move anyone, and machine output never looks like it is trying.

In 2001 two accounting researchers put a question to audit reviewers that firms had been living with for decades without writing it down. When a preparer documents a conclusion in the workpaper, does the reviewer form a judgment or inherit one? Tan and Yip-Ow published the answer in Contemporary Accounting Research, volume 18, issue 4, pages 663 to 678. Reviewers inherited. They were swayed by the conclusion the preparer had already written in.

That was not the useful part. The useful part was the condition under which the effect weakened. Reviewers leaned on the documented conclusion less when the memo was visibly stylized. When the shaping showed, when the memo read as a case being made rather than a record being kept, the reviewer pushed back. The anchor worked best when it looked neutral.

I have read that finding many times and I keep returning to the asymmetry, because the asymmetry is the whole problem in one sentence. A reviewer's defenses are triggered by signs of persuasion. Remove the signs and the defenses stay down, while the persuasion continues.

Where does the reviewer's picture of the work come from?

Two years earlier, Ricchiute had traced the same mechanism one level up the chain. In Accounting, Organizations and Society, volume 24, issue 2, pages 155 to 171, the paper showed that an audit senior's prior decision shapes what the senior documents, and that a partner who sees only that documented subset decides in the senior's direction. The partner is not reading the evidence. The partner is reading a selection of the evidence, and the selection was made by someone who had already decided.

Put the two papers together and you have a description of how a judgment travels through a file. The person closest to the evidence decides. The decision determines what gets written down. The written record arrives on the reviewer's desk looking like a neutral account. The reviewer, defenses down, defers to the conclusion already in it, and signs.

Nothing in that chain requires anyone to behave badly. The senior is not hiding anything. The partner is not lazy. The senior documented what seemed relevant, and what seemed relevant was shaped by what the senior had concluded. The literature established that much. What follows is my own inference from fifteen years of reading files, and it should be read as that.

What I think this looks like in practice is a review that cannot be distinguished from a read. The review note says reviewed, agreed. The conclusion in the signed document is a near-paraphrase of the preparer's conclusion with the hedging adjusted. Compare the memo as it arrived with the memo as it left. If the only difference is softer language, nobody reviewed anything. They read it, found it reasonable and moved on, and finding it reasonable is exactly what Tan and Yip-Ow would predict a reviewer to do with a neutral-looking memo.

Twenty-five years ago there was at least a tell. A memo that read like advocacy, that argued its case, that visibly wanted a particular answer, put the reviewer on guard. A reviewer could be trained to watch for that tell. The defense was not complete, but it existed, and it was attached to a signal that weak memos tended to give off.

On Monday. Before you open the conclusion section of anything you are reviewing, write down what you expect it to say and why. One line is enough. Then read. If your line and the preparer's conclusion match, you have corroboration. If they differ and you find yourself moving toward the preparer's view, you have the moment Tan and Yip-Ow measured, and you will be able to see it because you wrote your own view down first. A view you recorded before reading theirs is the only evidence you had one.

What does the tell look like when a machine wrote the memo?

There is no tell. That is the claim of this note, and it is an observation rather than a result, so take it as one.

Consider what a reviewer is handed when the analysis was generated. No author to argue with. No stake in the answer, or none the system will admit to. No rhetorical temperature, because the temperature is set by the product and it is set to calm. Uniform formatting, uniform confidence, uniform hedging, the same tone whether the substance is solid or hollow. It is the most neutral-looking document a reviewer will ever be handed, which by the 2001 finding puts it close to the most anchoring.

Twenty-five years separate that paper from this note. In those years the signal the profession learned to watch for was decoupled from the thing it signaled. A badly reasoned memo used to be badly organized, internally inconsistent or hedged into incoherence. It announced itself. Machine prose is well structured, confident and correctly formatted whether or not the reasoning underneath it holds. The early warning for weak work is gone, and nothing has replaced it.

I am wary of putting a name on the shortcut here, because the literature I would want to cite for it is not one I have settled on, so I will describe it instead. Ease of processing gets read as a signal of truth. Material that is easy to take in feels more likely to be right, independent of whether it is. A hedged correct answer now reads as weaker than a confident wrong one, and that is a reversal the profession has not adjusted to. The word I use for it in my own notes is fluency. I hold it as an observation from the seat, not as a named effect from the field.

The question I am working on now sits underneath this, and I want to be careful about how far along it is. It is in development. There are no results. The question is this: if a system is tuned on human approval, does it affirm the position its user has already stated, and is it most agreeable exactly where a reviewer most needs pushback? If the answer turns out to be yes, then the neutral-looking document is not only neutral-looking. It has read the reviewer's prompt and agreed with it, and the reviewer is now deferring to a reflection. I do not know yet. I know what it would look like in a file, because the prompt would contain the conclusion and the output would contain the prompt.

The closest experiment I can point to on reliance on artificial intelligence in audit is Commerford, Dennis, Joe and Ulla, published in the Journal of Accounting Research in 2022, volume 60, issue 1, pages 171 to 201, on auditor reliance on AI for complex estimates. It is four years old, and the systems I am describing are newer than it. I could not find a more recent experiment in a top audit journal that tests reliance on generated analysis, and I think that absence is worth saying out loud rather than papering over with “studies show.”

What I think this looks like in practice is a reviewer whose stated reason for accepting the work is about the output rather than the evidence. It is thorough. It is well laid out. It covers everything. None of those is a reason. Each is a description of how the document felt to read.

On Monday. Pick one claim in the generated analysis at random and trace it to source. Not the one that looks shaky. A random one. The thing being tested is the generator, not the sentence, and a sentence you selected because it looked weak tells you nothing about the ones that looked fine. If you cannot get from the claim to a document a third party could inspect, write that down as the finding, whatever the rest of the memo reads like.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming that the 2001 and 1999 results transfer to machine-generated analysis. They were experiments with human preparers and human reviewers, inside audit firms, a generation ago. The inference that a document with no author anchors harder than a document with one is mine, and it is the kind of inference I would want to see tested before I relied on it.

I am not claiming that auditors in general anchor. The direct evidence on that is older and more mixed than the way it usually gets cited. Kinney and Uecker in 1982 showed anchoring on client-provided unaudited book values in analytical review and on even odds in compliance evaluation, in The Accounting Review, volume 57, issue 1, pages 55 to 69. Joyce and Biddle, a year earlier in the Journal of Accounting Research, found departures from normative benchmarks that could not consistently be attributed to anchoring. I cite both because citing only the one that agrees with me would be the thing this note is about.

The open question is whether a review control can be built for a document that gives off no signal. Every defense I know of keys on something visible in the memo. I do not yet know what you key on when the memo is uniform by construction.
