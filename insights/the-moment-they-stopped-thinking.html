Field note · 16 November 2026

Nobody writes down the moment they stopped thinking.
How would we know?

The risk is deference, and deference leaves no finding, so the one control I know for it is a question rather than a procedure.

A finding needs three things. A condition that exists. A criterion the condition falls short of. Evidence that the condition existed on a date, in a file, where a second person can point to it. That skeleton has not changed in the fifteen years I have spent writing findings, and it will not change for the risk I am about to describe. The risk does not fit inside it.

Here is the risk, and I will say plainly that this is a hypothetical, because the real version is never recorded. It is six in the evening. A reviewer has been in the file since eight. An answer arrives from the tool she has been working alongside all afternoon. It is fluent. It is confident. It carries no provenance she can see, no author to argue with, no hedge to catch on. She reads it, finds nothing wrong with it and moves the conclusion into the working paper with the language softened a little. Nothing in that sequence is an error she could be written up for. She did not act on a wrong cue. She did not skip a step in the procedure. She stopped thinking, for about the length of one paragraph, and the file does not know.

The auditor everyone dreads teaches nobody. I believe that more each year, and the sentence underneath it is the harder one. You cannot examine your way out of deference, because nobody documents the moment they stopped thinking.

Why is there nothing to write up?

Take the skeleton and try to fill it in.

Condition. What exists? A conclusion in the working paper that matches the tool's output with hedging added. That is not a deficiency. It may be the right conclusion. Criterion. Which standard did it fall short of? The procedure said to review the output, and the output was reviewed in the sense that eyes passed over it and no exception was raised. Evidence. What would I attach? There is no artifact. What failed was a thought that did not occur, and the absence of a thought leaves no trace in a file.

The vocabulary for this is older than the tools. Parasuraman and Riley laid it out in 1997 in a paper whose title is the whole taxonomy: use, misuse, disuse, abuse. Misuse is their word for relying on an automated aid when the reliance was not warranted. Disuse is their word for rejecting an aid that should have been trusted. The reviewer at six in the evening is misuse, in exactly their sense. The term automation bias is a year older; Mosier and Skitka defined it in 1996 and split it into acting on a wrong automated cue and failing to act on what the automation never raised. Skitka, Mosier and Burdick then put evidence under it in 1999. People working with a highly but imperfectly reliable aid made more monitoring errors than people working with no aid at all. More, not fewer. The aid did not add a check. It displaced one.

Now look at what a control environment is built on. Most of what a second line and a third line do runs on findings. Findings feed issue logs. Issue logs feed remediation plans. Remediation plans feed the board report, and the board report is how the people who govern the institution learn what is failing inside it. Take away the finding and the whole apparatus reports green. Not because nothing is wrong. Because the thing that is wrong does not produce the input the apparatus runs on.

That is the problem with policing deference. There is nothing to police. An examiner can open the file, and the file is clean. A watchdog needs something to bite, and misuse of a fluent tool hands it nothing. I have fifteen years of findings behind me and not one of them says that a competent reviewer accepted a conclusion because it arrived well formed at the end of a long day. Not because it never happened. Because it never left a mark.

This is the question my doctoral work has turned toward, and I want to be exact about its status: it is in development and there are no results. When the analysis arrives already formed, does the reviewer still review it? I do not know yet. I do know that if the answer is no, the file will not be the thing that tells us.

On Monday. Stop asking the file whether the output was reviewed. Ask the reviewer what she expected before she opened it. If there is no recorded expectation, the review had nothing to compare against, and a comparison that was never made is not a control you can take credit for.

Can judgment be taught instead of policed?

Character cannot be taught in an afternoon and I would not try. A question can be, and the question is one. How would we know?

It does not ask whether the output is right. It asks what evidence would exist if the output were wrong, and whether anyone went looking for it. It is short enough to ask aloud in a room. It moves the burden from the output, which is fluent and will always look fine, to the world, which is where the risk actually lives. The most junior person present can ask it without accusing anyone of anything, which the dreaded auditor never could.

Here is what I would have a room actually do. This is a design, not a record of a session I have run, and I will not describe it as tested until it has been.

First, the words, because people cannot sort what they cannot name. Give the room Parasuraman and Riley's four terms and spend real time on the two in the middle. Then hand out a dozen short descriptions of reliance decisions, their own if they are willing to offer them, and have each one sorted into misuse or disuse. The loud cases sort themselves. The quiet ones start an argument, and the argument is the training.

Second, the expectation. Before anyone opens the tool, they write one line: what they expect the answer to be and what would make them change it. Then they open the tool. Then they compare. An expectation recorded before the output exists is the only evidence anyone will ever have that they were not moved by it. It costs a minute. I include myself among the people who skip it.

Third, the question itself, asked of an output they have already accepted. Not whether it is right. How would we know if it were not? What would be sitting in the ledger, in the population, in the control evidence, that the output would not have mentioned? Who would notice, and when? If the honest answer is nobody until the next cycle, that is the finding the file was never going to write, said aloud where it can be heard.

Fourth, the cleared population. Everything the tool flagged has a person on it. Nothing the tool cleared does. Have the room pick one item the tool passed and take it to source, all the way down. Not the weakest-looking item. A random one. What is being tested is the reliance, not the sentence, and an item chosen because it looked shaky says nothing about the ones that looked fine.

None of this makes anyone dread the auditor. It is the opposite of dread. It gives competent, tired professionals the words for a mistake they make, and the one question that interrupts it. The alternative is to wait for a finding that will never arrive and call the silence assurance.

On Monday. Put the question in the procedure, in those four words, at the step where the output is accepted. Not as a checkbox. As a line the reviewer has to fill in: how would we know if this were wrong, and what did I look at to find out. A blank in that line is the only artifact deference will ever leave.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming the tools are wrong more often than people are. I have no evidence for that, and the question is not about accuracy. Suppose a tool is right almost every time and is deferred to every time. The gap between those two rates is a set of unreviewed errors in a clean file, and the clean file is my concern.

I am not claiming the training works. It is a design I would run and measure. No results exist and I will not describe any until they do.

The question I cannot resolve is whether one question survives contact with six in the evening. A procedure can require the line to be filled in. It cannot require the person filling it to mean it, and a line filled in from habit is a second fluent answer sitting on top of the first. I think the answer is that the question has to be asked by a person, out loud, to another person, and that the room matters more than the form. I do not know how to evidence that either.
