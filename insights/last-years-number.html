Field note · 12 October 2026

This year's evidence,
or last year's number?

Every recurring engagement starts with a figure nobody in the room produced this year, and the audit literature has been testing what that does to judgment since 1981.

The prior-year working paper opens first. It always does. Before anyone has looked at a single piece of this year's evidence, the balance that passed last year, the threshold that was set last year and the conclusion that was signed last year are on the screen. That is efficient. It is also the condition under which one particular shortcut operates, and the shortcut has a name and a literature, and the literature is less tidy than the version of it that gets quoted.

Tversky and Kahneman described anchoring and adjustment in 1974 (Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131). An initial value captures the estimate, and adjustment away from it is insufficient, even when the anchor is known to be arbitrary. In a recurring engagement the anchor is not arbitrary. It is last year's number, and I think that is the harder case, because an anchor that is also plausible gives the reviewer no reason to move at all.

What did the audit literature actually establish?

Two papers carry most of the weight when anchoring in auditors is cited, and they do not say the same thing.

Kinney and Uecker published in The Accounting Review in 1982 (57(1), 55–69). They found that auditors anchor on client-provided unaudited book values when performing analytical review, and on even odds when evaluating compliance. Read the first half as an examiner. The client hands over an unaudited figure. The auditor's expectation for the audited figure forms around it. The evidence that would move the expectation arrives afterward and moves it less than it should. What I think that looks like in a recurring engagement is the same shape with a different actor, the client's book value playing the part that last year's number plays every other year. The paper's title promises mitigation. I am citing it for the anchoring, not for the remedy.

Joyce and Biddle published in the Journal of Accounting Research a year earlier (1981, 19(1), 120–145), and their result is mixed. Auditors departed from normative benchmarks, but the departures could not consistently be attributed to anchoring. It is easy to cite that paper as if it found that auditors anchor. It did not. It found that auditors were off the benchmark, and it could not consistently say that anchoring was why.

I want to stay on the mixed result, because it is the point. A field that has been studying anchoring in its own practitioners for forty-five years has one clean finding and one honest shrug, and the shrug is the more useful document. It tells you the effect is real enough to find in one task and hard enough to isolate that a careful experiment could not pin it in another. Anyone who tells you the research shows auditors anchor has anchored on the clean half. The sentence is not false. It is the adjustment that is insufficient.

What I think this looks like in a file is inference from fifteen years of findings, not a citation. The conclusion in this year's working paper is a near-paraphrase of last year's, with this year's figures substituted in and a sentence of hedging added. Look at what changed between the two. If the only difference is the numbers and the softer language, nobody formed an expectation this year. They inherited one.

On Monday. Before the prior-year file is opened, write down the expectation. The range you would accept for the balance, the rate you would expect for exceptions, the conclusion you would reach if this year's evidence were all you had. Date it and put it in the file. An expectation recorded before the anchor arrived is the only evidence that you were not moved by it. It costs ten minutes and almost nobody does it.

Which of the eight mitigants can an examiner evidence?

The study I described last week was built around eight antecedents, the conditions under which judgment in a recurring engagement is supposed to follow current evidence instead of the inherited figure. They are training and awareness, auditor rotation, analytical tools, structured processes, feedback and reflection, independent review, regulatory guidance and performance incentives. The model runs those through the quality of the auditor's judgment and the rigor of the audit process to the outcome it cares about, which is a judgment driven by current evidence rather than last year's number. Fifty-five items measure it. The sample did not arrive, so the model is specified and fielded and not validated, and I will keep saying that until it is.

Read the eight as an examiner would, which means asking of each one what artifact would prove it operated last quarter.

Four of them leave artifacts without being asked. Auditor rotation produces a roster with names and dates. Independent review produces a sign-off with a name and a date, and the name is not the preparer's. Structured processes produce a template, a checklist, a methodology under version control. Regulatory guidance produces a citation in the methodology and a mapping to it. Those are controls in the sense I use the word. They can be tested, they can fail and the failure is visible.

Three of them are culture, and culture does not leave an artifact unless somebody engineers one. Training and awareness produces an attendance record, which evidences attendance. Feedback and reflection produces nothing at all in the ordinary course, because nobody documents the moment they stopped thinking. Performance incentives produce a compensation plan, which evidences what was paid, not what it did to a judgment. I am not saying these are weaker. Whether they carry more of the weight than the four with artifacts is exactly the thing an unvalidated model cannot tell you. I am saying that when an examiner asks for evidence of them, the honest answer is usually a description, and a description is what you write when there is no artifact.

That leaves the eighth, and it is the one I now read differently. Analytical tools sit in the model as a mitigant. The premise, as I understand it, holds for a tool that computes. A tool that builds an expectation from this year's data gives the auditor something other than last year's number to form a judgment around.

Now consider a tool that writes. System-generated analysis arrives already formed. It has a conclusion, a rationale and a confident register, and it arrives before the reviewer has formed anything of their own. In the recurring engagement the anchor used to arrive once a year, in the prior-year file. It now arrives every morning, in the draft. The output becomes the anchor. That is the first of three links I am working on in the current research, and I want to be exact about its status. It is in development. There are no results. It is a question about whether the mitigant in the model has become the mechanism the model was built to counter, and I do not know the answer. Whether the fifty-five items can even see that difference is a question for the instrument itself [UNVERIFIED: whether any of the fifty-five items distinguishes analysis the auditor computed from analysis a system produced].

On Monday. Take the list of mitigants your function claims against anchoring, whatever it calls them, and write next to each the artifact that proves it operated last quarter. Where there is no artifact, you have a value, which is fine, and not a control, which should be said. Then take the analytical tool and ask one question of its last twelve outputs. How many times did the signed conclusion differ from the tool's? If the answer is none, the tool is not mitigating the anchor. It is the anchor, delivered daily.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming the eight antecedents mitigate anything. The model was specified to test whether they do, and it has not been tested, because the population it needed could not be reached through standard research infrastructure. Last week's note has the count. Feasibility, not validation.

I am not claiming that system-generated analysis anchors reviewers more than a prior-year figure does. That is the question. The audit literature on reliance on AI is thinner than it should be. The canonical experiment is Commerford, Dennis, Joe and Ulla in the Journal of Accounting Research in 2022 (60(1), 171–201), on auditor reliance on AI for complex estimates. A search for a more recent experiment in a top audit journal on reliance on AI-generated analysis did not find one, which is itself worth saying out loud.

The open question is the one I cannot design around yet. The literature's anchor was a number. A client's book value, an even-odds prior. Adjustment away from a number can be measured, because the distance is a number too. The anchor I am now looking at is a paragraph. I do not know how to measure adjustment away from a paragraph, and until I do, the claim that the output becomes the anchor is a hypothesis, and I am still working out what instrument would test it.
