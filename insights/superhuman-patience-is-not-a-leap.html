Field note · 23 November 2026

Superhuman patience is not a leap.
What was the work meant to achieve?

One mathematician reviewed a machine's proof the way he would review a colleague's, and what he found says more about review than it does about machines.

In August 2026 a Cambridge mathematician was handed a proof produced by a model and did the ordinary thing. He engaged with it, in his words, as he would if a human had written it.

The proof was one of ten that OpenAI published on 1 August 2026, each with a formal verification in Lean, in a public repository. I wrote about the manifest in September. This note is about what happened when a person read past it.

What does a real review actually find?

Francesco Fournier-Facio's account is in Scientific American's report of 6 August 2026. Reading the proof as he would read any colleague's, he found that the key step was not new. It combined ideas from two papers already in the literature, one published in 2016 and one in 2019. The result held. The route to it had been on the shelf for years, in two pieces.

Scientific American's summary of the capability is the sentence I keep returning to. What the system showed was superhuman patience for assembling puzzle pieces, not the ability to make some profound intellectual leap. Note what that sentence does not say. It does not say the proof was wrong. Correctness was never contested.

A second reader was blunter. In a statement dated 7 September 2026, Tristan Buckmaster of New York University said that the first LLM-generated proof he was sent was the most horrendous he had ever read, and that one of three papers could only be described as AI slop. [UNVERIFIED: which papers the statement refers to, and whether any is among the ten.] Set the two readings side by side. A proof can be correct and horrendous to read. A proof can be correct and read like a leap until someone with the right background traces it back to 2016 and 2019. Neither the ease nor the difficulty of reading told either reviewer anything about whether the mathematics held.

That is the observation I want to put on the record, and I will put it as an observation, because I have not settled on a reference for it and will not dress it as a named effect. Material that is easy to take in feels true. Material that is hard to take in feels weak. Both feelings arrive before any checking has happened, and both are about the page rather than the world. A language model produces text that is structured, confident and correctly formatted whether or not the substance holds. The signal that used to warn us about weak work has come loose from the weakness. Here it pointed both ways at once, at correct work, and the only thing that settled anything was a person reading all the way down.

What Fournier-Facio did has a shape I recognize. He did not ask whether the output was impressive. He asked where each step came from. That question has a checkable answer, and the answer turned the result from a leap into an assembly without changing a line of it. That is what a review is. Not a verdict on the output. A reconstruction of its provenance, which the output cannot supply about itself.

On Monday. When an output arrives and the reason for accepting it is about the output, that it is thorough, well laid out, covers everything, treat that as no reason at all. Pick one claim at random, not the weakest-looking one, and take it to source. A claim chosen because it looked shaky tests the sentence. A claim chosen at random tests the generator.

What was the work meant to achieve?

On 11 September 2026 twenty-five Fields Medalists, Terence Tao among them, signed a declaration at mathandai.org titled A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics. It argues that the push by AI companies to solve mathematical problems as a benchmark is detrimental to the science of mathematics. The line that carries it is this: the mass production at faster and faster pace of 'true/false' statements could destroy fertile ground instead of breathing life into new ideas. It does not dispute correctness. It closes on a question: how to make sure that, as AI changes the way work is done, we do not lose sight of what that work was meant to achieve.

I am not a mathematician and I will not referee their argument. I am going to take their question literally and ask it of my own field.

Assembling pieces is most of audit work. Tie the balance to the ledger. Vouch the sample to the invoice. Trace the transaction to the approval. Read the control narrative against the walkthrough. Match this year's analytical to last year's and explain the variance. All of it is patient and checkable, and a system with superhuman patience will do it faster and with fewer transcription errors than I ever did. If that is what the work was meant to achieve, the work is nearly finished and the profession should say so.

It is not what the work was meant to achieve. The pieces are assembled so that a person can form a judgment about whether the evidence is sufficient and appropriate for the conclusion the engagement set out to test. That sentence contains the part only judgment does, and it is small. Deciding what the pieces have to add up to before they are assembled. Deciding which population to look at and which to leave alone. Deciding when enough is enough. Deciding whether the thing that has been assembled is the thing the engagement was actually about, or something adjacent that happened to be easier to assemble.

Here is my concern about that last one, and it is inference from my own files rather than anything the literature has established. Ricchiute showed in 1999 that audit seniors' prior decisions shape what gets documented, and that partners who see only that documented subset decide in the senior's direction. The partner's judgment is real, but it operates on a record someone else assembled, and the assembly carried a direction. Replace the senior with a system of superhuman patience and the subset is chosen by the system. The partner still signs, still in the direction of the assembly, and nothing in the file says that the direction was never a decision anyone made.

So the question for a review function is not whether to let the machine assemble. It will, and it should. The question is how you would evidence the part only judgment does, once the assembly no longer leaves any human fingerprints to distinguish it from. I think the answer has three parts and I hold them loosely. An expectation, written before the pieces are assembled, of what they should add up to. A recorded alternative, the conclusion that was considered and rejected and the reason. And one test chosen to disconfirm rather than to confirm, selected by a person, with the selection explained. None of those is produced by patience. Each is a trace of a person deciding what the work was for.

This is the question my doctoral research has turned toward, and its status is exactly this: in development, no results. When the analysis arrives already assembled, does the reviewer still review it, and if so, what in the file proves it? I do not know. I know that twenty-five people who do the hardest checking there is just told their own field that correctness is not the point, and they are owed a hearing in mine.

On Monday. Open a recent file and find the sentence that could only have been written by a person who knew what the engagement was for. The expectation set before the evidence arrived. The alternative rejected. The test chosen to break the conclusion rather than support it. If you cannot find it, the file records assembly, and assembly is the part that is about to be done faster by something else.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming the proofs are wrong. They are right as far as anyone has said, and the formal verification behind them is an advance my own field should want more of. I am not claiming that assembling pieces is lesser work. Fournier-Facio's reading was itself an assembly, of the proof against two papers he knew, and it was the most valuable act in the whole episode.

I am not claiming to know how to evidence judgment. The three traces above are my best guess, they are untested and the research that would test them has no results yet. I do not know whether the distinction survives a close look. It may be that judgment is just assembly with a longer memory, and that superhuman patience eventually covers it. If that is true I would rather find out than assume otherwise.

The question I am left with is the Medalists' own, turned on a review function. As this changes the way the work is done, what is the record that shows we did not lose sight of what the work was meant to achieve? I can describe what its absence looks like. I cannot yet describe its presence in a file, and until I can, nobody should take my word that it is there.

If something here is wrong, overstated or too confident, tell me: YasirAMalik@gmail.com. I would rather be corrected than agreed with.
