Before we automate,
how would we know?

A compelling growth story is a starting hypothesis. It is not yet a business case.

For an owner considering another location, a team exploring AI, or a school designing an employer project, I would start with the same question: what decision are we trying to improve—and what evidence would tell us we were wrong?

One problem. One baseline. One accountable owner.

“We need AI” is not a sufficiently defined problem. “Our managers spend six hours rebuilding a report, and errors delay purchasing decisions” gives us something to investigate. First verify the baseline. Then decide whether process redesign, training, better controls or automation is the right intervention.

My proposed discovery approach has three stages: understand the operating reality, scope a bounded pilot, and expand only after reviewing results. Agree the outcome, the risks and a stop condition before implementation. A pilot that disproves the original idea can still be a useful result.

A hospitality example: readiness before expansion

A growing neighborhood can make an expansion conversation worth having. It cannot establish demand, acceptable occupancy costs or repeatable store economics on its own. Test customer behavior, staffing, operating consistency and site economics. Keep projected demand separate from observed demand.

A practical first project might be documenting a manager workflow or testing a repeatable training module. It is not a promise of franchise readiness. Legal, accounting and other specialist work would require appropriately qualified professionals under separate scopes.

A school collaboration should begin with learning

Hospitality students could examine service operations; business students could test a unit-economics model; a technology group could assess a limited automation prototype. Those are possible project designs—not announced programs. Any collaboration needs a willing sponsor, learning outcomes, institutional approvals and suitable safeguards before students or organizational data become involved.

Keep the claims and the mechanisms separate

In In the Absence of Reason, Jennifer Kinne cautions against treating similar human and AI outputs as evidence of the same underlying mechanism. My practical takeaway is to ask what was actually observed, what explanation is being proposed, and what would distinguish competing explanations.

PilotWave Holdings describes a staged approach to AI-enabled business transformation. The relevant inspiration here is sequencing diagnosis and implementation—not adopting its investment model, performance claims or branding.

My FIU DBA profile describes my work in audit, risk and AI governance and research into cognitive bias in audit judgment. That is the lens I bring to these conversations; it does not make a proposed project an FIU initiative.

Turn the idea into a useful conversation

The discovery form asks for the problem, intended outcome, current evidence, assumptions and constraints. It generates a brief you can review before sending. The next step is a 1:1, followed—only if useful—by a scoped discovery session with agreed participants.
