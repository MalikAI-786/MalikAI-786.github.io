Field note · 7 December 2026

What would you
show me first?

When a firm says its AI is governed, the next sentence from the examiner's seat is a request for a document. The order of the requests is the method.

"We have a policy."

Said early in a conversation about AI use, that sentence ends the conversation badly, and not because policies are worthless. It ends badly because the firm has just told the examiner what it thinks the evidence is. A policy is a statement of intent. It describes what the firm meant to happen. The examination is about what happened, and the two are different documents.

Nothing that follows describes a particular institution or a particular examination. It is the order I would ask in, and why the order matters more than the list.

What do you ask for, and in what order?

Three things, in sequence, and the sequence is not negotiable because each one scopes the next.

First, the inventory. Not the approved list, and not the list in the policy. The list of where these systems are actually in use today, with the date it was last reconciled against what is running. An inventory that lists what was approved describes intent again, in table form. The question is whether anyone has walked the floor. If the inventory is aspirational, everything downstream of it is governed in principle and ungoverned in fact, and the firm does not know which parts are which.

Second, for each output in that inventory that reaches a decision, the named accountable reviewer. A name, not a role. Not the process owner, and not the committee. The person answerable for that specific conclusion, written down before the work was done rather than assigned afterward. If the answer is "the second line reviews it," the answer is a function, and a function cannot be asked who verified a particular output on a particular day. The name is the control. Its absence is the finding.

Third, the documentation that survives someone being allowed to disagree. Evidence that the review has, at least once, changed what the system produced, with the before and the after in the file. A review that has never said no has no evidence it can. This is the request that separates a review from a step, and it is the one most firms are least ready for, because the first two can be produced from a spreadsheet.

Here is why the policy ends the conversation badly. The finding I wrote most often across fifteen years in internal audit was not a missing control. It was a control nobody could evidence had operated. The policy existed. The control was described, assigned and approved. What did not exist was any artifact showing it had run on a given day for a given item, and without that artifact the control and its absence look identical in the file. Everything about the three requests above is designed to get past that. The inventory shows where the control was supposed to run. The named reviewer shows who was supposed to run it. The surviving disagreement shows that, at least once, it did.

Two frameworks sit behind that list, and I will say exactly how much of each I am using. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is what I referenced, alongside federal model risk guidance, when I proposed an AI governance framework for an internal governance team inside a large bank. I map to it. I am not quoting it here. The second is SR 26-2, the revised interagency guidance on model risk management, issued on 17 April 2026, which supersedes SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. It is most relevant to banking organizations above $30 billion in total assets, and it says that the quality of validation depends on the rigor and effectiveness of the review rather than on organizational structure. That sentence is the third request in supervisory language. A review is evidenced by what it did, not by where it sits on the chart.

Whether any particular generative tool falls inside SR 26-2's definition of a model is a question I am not going to answer from memory. [UNVERIFIED: SR 26-2's scope language on generative and agentic AI, not confirmed against the published text] The three requests do not depend on the answer. A firm that cannot produce an inventory, a name and a surviving disagreement has a governance gap whether or not a supervisory letter happens to reach it.

On Monday. Before the meeting, write the three requests on one page in that order and do not accept a substitution for any of them. When the policy is offered, take it, say thank you and ask for the reconciliation date of the inventory. If the inventory has no reconciliation date, that is the first finding, and it was found in the first ten minutes.

Why does the risk assessment look like the tool's output?

There is a fourth document, and it belongs to a different mechanism.

Put the firm's current AI risk assessment next to the most common outputs of the tooling the firm uses to write it. Risk assessments tend to converge on what the tooling describes well. The risks that are fluently generated are the risks that appear. The risks the system has no good vocabulary for, because they are specific to this firm, or because they live in the gap between two systems rather than inside either, tend not to appear. Not because they were assessed and dismissed. Because they never reached the table.

The mechanism is availability. Tversky and Kahneman described it in 1974 in Science, volume 185, number 4157, pages 1124 to 1131: frequency and likelihood are judged by how readily instances come to mind. A risk assessment is a frequency and likelihood exercise performed by people, and the instances that come readily to mind are now, for many teams, the instances the tooling put there. What I think this looks like in practice, and this is my inference rather than the paper's, is a plan whose coverage has drifted toward whatever the system surfaces first, written by people who experienced that drift as thoroughness.

The tell is the comparison itself. Lay the risk assessment beside the tool's most common outputs. If they match closely, the firm will read that as agreement. It may be an echo. Convergence between a document and the system that helped write it is not two sources agreeing. It is one source, read twice.

The counter-move is cheap and it produces an artifact, which is the only kind of counter-move I trust. Build part of the risk assessment before consulting the tooling. Keep that list, dated. Then compare it with the final plan and write down what fell off between the two. A risk that was on the human list and is absent from the final plan was either assessed and dismissed, in which case the dismissal should be in the file, or it was quietly displaced by something the system found easier to say. The firm cannot tell those apart without the first list, and neither can I.

The fourth request is the third request again. The pre-tooling list is the documentation that survives disagreement, applied to the plan rather than to a single output. A firm that has it can show that its risk coverage was not the tool's coverage with a logo on it. A firm that does not have it cannot, and I will not infer it from the quality of the final document, because that quality is the thing in question.

On Monday. Ask for the first draft of the risk assessment, before tooling, and the final. If there is no first draft, ask the team to produce one now, from memory, in a room without the system, and compare. What is on the human list and missing from the plan is the agenda for the rest of the examination.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming firms that answer "we have a policy" are hiding anything. Most are telling the truth about the only artifact anyone asked for before. I am not claiming SR 26-2 reaches every firm or every tool, and I have marked the one scope question I could not confirm against the text. I am not claiming a risk assessment that resembles the tool's output is wrong. It may be exactly right. The resemblance is not evidence that it is, and the firm needs a separate way to show it.

The research behind this is in development and has no results. The three requests come from fifteen years of writing the same finding, not from a study, and should be read as one examiner's method rather than as anything the field has established.

The open question is the one I cannot close from the examiner's seat. The risk that never reached the table leaves no artifact. There is no memo about the thing nobody raised. The pre-tooling list catches what was raised and then lost. It does not catch what was never available to anyone in the room, and I do not yet know what evidence of that absence would look like. I know what it looks like when a plan has drifted. I do not know how to show that one has not.
