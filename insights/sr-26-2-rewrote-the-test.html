Field note · 2 November 2026

Effective challenge was rewritten.
What does it require now?

In April the agencies replaced the definition of effective challenge. Most of the industry read a restatement. It is a rewrite, and AI-assisted review is where the difference will be tested.

On 17 April 2026 the Federal Reserve issued SR 26-2, "Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management," interagency, with the OCC's copy carried as Bulletin 2026-13. It supersedes SR 11-7, which had governed model risk since 2011, and SR 21-8 from 2021. On a first pass the effective challenge passage reads like the old one with the nouns refreshed.

It is not. SR 11-7 said effective challenge depended on incentives, competence and influence. SR 26-2 defines it as critical analysis conducted by objective experts who evaluate model risk and effect appropriate changes throughout the model lifecycle, performed by people with appropriate expertise, sufficient independence to maintain objectivity and the organizational standing and influence to effect change. Incentives left the triad. Independence arrived. Competence became expertise, which is a higher bar and a more specific one.

Then there is the sentence I would put on the wall of any validation function. Validation is now risk-based rather than fixed-cycle, and its quality "depends on the rigor and effectiveness of the review rather than on organizational structure."

Why is a rewrite different from a restatement?

Under the old wording a bank could evidence effective challenge structurally. A separate reporting line. A separate budget. A validation group that sat outside the business and reviewed on a schedule. The organization chart was the evidence, and in fifteen years of audit and risk work I saw a great deal of effort go into making the chart say the right thing.

The new text takes the chart off the table as proof. Independence survives, but as a property of the reviewer's objectivity rather than of where the box sits. And the rigor sentence cuts in both directions. A structurally independent validation group that does shallow work no longer satisfies it. A review that is rigorous and effective can count even where the structure is thin. The guidance moved the test from the shape of the function to the quality of the work, and that is a different thing to examine.

The guidance says it is most relevant to banking organizations above $30 billion in total assets. I examine state-chartered banks for the Florida Office of Financial Regulation, and the test does not stop being useful below that line. A three-part question about who reviewed something, what they knew and what they could change is a question I can put to any institution of any size, and I intend to.

What changes is what I would ask for. Under SR 11-7 the natural request was the chart and the validation schedule. Under SR 26-2 the natural request is a validation report with four things in it: who reviewed the model, what expertise they brought, in what sense they were independent of the output and what they changed as a result. If the report answers the first and not the last three, it has evidenced a structure.

On Monday. Take one validation report from this year. Strike every sentence that describes structure: who reports to whom, which committee received it, which cycle it fell in. What remains is what the guidance now scores. If nothing remains, the report evidenced a chart, and a chart is no longer the test.

Does AI-assisted review pass the three-part test?

Review functions are putting models into the review chain, and I am not writing from the outside of that. In Citi's audit function I built and deployed a retrieval-augmented assistant that took roughly a third off review cycle time. I am not against the tool. I am describing what it does to the chain, because the three-part test is the sharpest instrument I know for seeing it.

Expertise first. When the analyst drafts a finding with the assistant and the reviewer checks the finding with the same assistant, the second pass carries every blind spot of the first. That is not independent expertise. It is the same expertise applied twice, and a chain that counts it twice has one reviewer, not two. The guidance asks for appropriate expertise in the people doing the challenge, and the question an examiner can put is simple. What did the reviewer know that the tool did not.

Independence second. The guidance means independence sufficient to maintain objectivity about the output. The direction my doctoral research is pointed at is whether a system tuned on human approval tends to affirm the position it is handed, and whether it is most agreeable exactly where a reviewer most needs pushback. That work is in development and I have no results. I have the question, and the question is enough to make independence the part I would test first. A reviewer whose objectivity depends on a system that may be shaped to agree has not established independence. It has assumed it.

Standing third, and this is the one that settles it. The guidance asks for the organizational standing and influence to effect change. An assistant cannot refuse to sign. It cannot hold a release, escalate to an audit committee or tell a managing director the number is wrong and stay in the room while that lands. A reviewer that cannot decline to ship has no standing, and no amount of capability substitutes for it. What it performs is a step. What the guidance requires is a challenge.

Underneath all three is a mechanism I do not have a settled name for in the literature, and I am not going to borrow one. What I see in review chains is this. Add a participant and responsibility for the outcome spreads thinner across everyone in it. The analyst assumes the reviewer will verify. The reviewer assumes the analyst confirmed what the tool produced. The approver assumes the tool was validated, and in a sense it was, by a group whose chart says independent. Every person in that chain behaved reasonably and nothing was checked. I think of it as an accountability gap, and it is the failure I find hardest to write up, because it is nobody's error.

Read SR 26-2 again with that in mind and the rewrite stops looking like housekeeping. A test that accepts structure as evidence is a test the gap passes, because structure is exactly what each participant points to when asked who checked. A test that scores the rigor and effectiveness of the review is a test the gap fails, because rigor has to have been done by someone, and that someone can be named.

On Monday. For every AI-assisted conclusion in the file, put the three parts in the workpaper as three lines before the work starts. Who brought the expertise. In what sense they were independent of the output. What they had the standing to stop. If the third line names a tool, you do not have a third line. Then ask three people in the chain who verified the conclusion. Three different names, none of them the speaker's own, is the finding.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming SR 26-2 says anything specific about AI systems in review. The text I have verified is the effective challenge definition and the validation sentence, and I am applying them as a test to something the guidance may not name. If it does, I would rather hear it from the text than from a summary of the text.

I am not claiming structural independence was ever worthless. A separate reporting line is a precondition for objectivity more often than not. The point is narrower. It was being accepted as the proof, and the proof has moved.

The part I cannot resolve is the second test. When the author of an analysis and its reviewer used the same system, I do not yet know what evidence of the reviewer's independence from the output would look like. I know what its absence looks like, because that is the file where the signed conclusion is a paraphrase of the tool's draft with softer language. I do not yet know what its presence looks like, and the guidance does not tell me.
