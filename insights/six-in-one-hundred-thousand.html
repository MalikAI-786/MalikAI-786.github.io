Field note · 5 October 2026

Six in one hundred thousand.
Who was in the survey of auditors?

A study built to measure anchoring in auditors screened 330,000 people and found roughly twenty it could use, and the number is the finding.

The instrument was finished before the sample was. Fifty-five items across eleven constructs. Sixteen hypotheses. An IRB approval on file and an analytic protocol written out from exploratory factor analysis through structural modeling. The question it was built to answer is a plain one. In a recurring engagement, does this year's judgment follow this year's evidence, or last year's number?

Then the screen ran, across 330,000 research-panel participants. Roughly twenty met the study's eligibility criteria. That is about six in one hundred thousand, and it is not a sample. It is a count of how many of the people the research infrastructure can reach are the people the study needed.

The study has been reframed as a feasibility study, and that word is doing exact work. It did not validate anything. It established what it is feasible to reach, which turned out to be the more useful thing to know.

What did you buy when you bought a survey of auditors?

Somewhere in most functions there is a slide that cites a survey. Pick a number; four hundred audit professionals, say, or a thousand examiners. The figure on the slide is the sample size. The figure that was never on the slide is the one above it. How many people were screened to produce that sample, and what the screen was.

Read the slide against six in one hundred thousand. A research panel is a population of people who signed up to answer surveys. It is enormous and it is cheap to reach, and for most questions about most people that is enough. For the specialist population this study needed, the panel contained almost nobody. Not at a discount. Not at a premium. The finding is that specialist professional populations cannot be reached through standard research infrastructure at any price, and the last three words are the part that surprised me. I had assumed reach was a budget line. It is a property of the infrastructure.

So the first question for any survey of a specialist population is not what the respondents said. It is who answered. If the vendor screened a general panel and still reports hundreds of auditors, one of two things happened. The screen was looser than yours, or the population on the slide is not the population that answered. Either way the sample size is answering a question nobody asked.

The shortcut underneath this has a name. Tversky and Kahneman called it availability in 1974 (Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131). Frequency and likelihood are judged by how readily instances come to mind. What I think it looks like in practice is this. The sample you can reach gets mistaken for the population you need, and the mistake is invisible at the point of purchase, because the reachable sample is the one that comes to mind. I made the same error on my own study. I spent fifteen years among auditors. They are available to me in exactly the sense Tversky and Kahneman meant, and that made them feel common. Against a panel of 330,000 they are six in one hundred thousand.

On Monday. When a survey of auditors, examiners or risk officers lands on your desk, ask for two numbers before you read a single result. How many people were screened, and how many passed. Then ask for the screen itself. If the vendor can give you the sample size but not the screen-out count, what you have is the people who were available, and the slide should say so.

What goes in the record when the study does not go to plan?

The qualifying examination was in July 2026. The study I presented was not the study I designed. The one I designed had fifty-five items, eleven constructs and sixteen hypotheses, and it was going to run from exploratory factor analysis through structural modeling on a real sample. The one I presented had the same fifty-five items, the same eleven constructs and the same sixteen hypotheses, with roughly twenty eligible participants and the word feasibility in front of it.

The committee's final evaluation included a line I have kept. "Your reflection on the challenges encountered during the study… demonstrates sound research judgment." That is Dr. Juan C. Rey of the FIU College of Business, and the thing he is crediting is not the result. There was no result in the sense the design promised. He is crediting the account of what went wrong.

I have read a great many files in which nothing went wrong. A control inventory with no retired controls. An issue log where every issue closed on its due date. A testing plan executed exactly as written, every quarter, for years. None of those is a record. A record that contains only successes is a record of what the author chose to write down, and the first thing an examiner does with it is ask what is missing.

There were ways to make twenty become four hundred. Loosen the screen. Drop a criterion. Take the panel's word for who is an auditor. Each of those produces a dataset, and each of those loses the population, and the fifty-five items would have measured something about people who were not the people the question is about. The reframing was the alternative. Report the count. Report the ratio. State what the ratio says about the infrastructure and what it does to the design of the next study. It is the less impressive document and it is the only honest one.

The constraint is itself evidence. That sentence took me longer to accept than any result would have, because a constraint does not look like a finding. It looks like an apology. It is not. A study that discovers its population cannot be reached by the ordinary means has discovered something about every prior study that claims to have reached it by those means, and that is worth more to the field than a validated model on the wrong people.

On Monday. Open your own function's record for the last twelve months and count the entries that record something that did not work. A scope that was reduced. A test that could not be performed. A population that could not be reached. If the count is zero, the record is not complete, and the next person to read it will reach that conclusion faster than you did. Write the failed thing in the same font, in the same place and with the same numbers as the things that worked.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming the study measured anchoring. It did not reach the sample that would have let it try, and the instrument stands fielded, not validated. Nothing from any participant appears here. The counts above are screening counts, not responses.

I am not claiming research panels are the wrong tool. For most populations they are the right one and the cheap one. The claim is narrower. For a specialist professional population, the screen-out rate is a finding, and a study that reports the sample without the screen has left its most informative number out.

What I do not know is how the population gets reached at all. The infrastructure that exists for recruiting research participants does not contain these people, and the next design has to find them some other way. I have not found it yet. If you have run a study on auditors or examiners that reached the population it needed, I would like to know how, and what it cost.

If something here is wrong, overstated or too confident, tell me: YasirAMalik@gmail.com. I would rather be corrected than agreed with.
