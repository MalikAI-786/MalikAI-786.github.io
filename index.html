Research

Cognitive bias in audit judgment.

Doctoral research at Florida International University. Qualifying examination completed and passed, July 2026.

Mitigating Anchoring Bias in Long-Term Auditor Engagements — an eleven-construct, fifty-five-item measurement model with sixteen hypotheses, grounded in dual-process theory and the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic. IRB-approved (IRB-25-0462), instrument fielded, with a complete analytic protocol specified from exploratory factor analysis through structural modeling.

The question underneath it is simple to state and hard to defend against: in a recurring engagement, does this year's judgment follow this year's evidence — or last year's number?

Current direction — AI and professional judgment

In development. How large language models interact with professional judgment in recurring review work. The chain under investigation:

Link 01 · The anchor

The output becomes the anchor

System-generated analysis is now the starting number — automated, continuous, and delivered at a scale no prior-year workpaper could match.

Link 02 · Sycophancy

The model confirms rather than challenges

Tuned on human approval, a system tends to affirm the position a user has already stated. It is most agreeable exactly where a reviewer most needs pushback.

Link 03 · Epistemic drift

Conclusions converge on each other

As successive models reprocess the same work, conclusions converge on one another rather than on evidence — and the record, written with the same tools, remembers the drift as consensus.

Stated plainly, that is a control problem, not just a technology problem: a system that agrees with you, feeding a record that remembers the agreement. The work is to treat professional skepticism as something a control environment has to actively defend — and to test which design choices hold the line without discarding what these tools genuinely do well.

Reflection · Sufism through Iqbal

Khudi, and the discipline of not dissolving.

Why a bank examiner keeps a philosopher on the desk.

Iqbal took Rumi as his guide and then spent a lifetime arguing with the tradition that raised him. The strand of mysticism that counsels withdrawal from the world — that treats the self as something to be thinned out and finally erased — he could not accept. Against it he set khudi: selfhood, not as ego or appetite, but as the disciplined fortification of the individual against everything that would flatten it.

I did not come to this as a scholar. I came to it the way most Lahori schoolboys do — recited at assembly, half understood, filed away. It took twenty years of professional life to see what he was actually asking for.

Because professional skepticism is khudi in a control environment. An auditor's entire value is a refusal to dissolve: into the client's framing, into last year's number, into the confident consensus of a room that has already decided. Independence is not a reporting line on an organisational chart. It is a condition of the self, and it has to be actively held.

Which is why the current moment interests me so much. We have built systems that are fluent, tireless, and tuned to agree — most agreeable, as it happens, exactly where a reviewer most needs to be contradicted. Iqbal warned against a spirituality that dissolves the self into a comfortable infinite. I spend my working life on a narrower version of the same worry: a profession dissolving its judgment into a machine that flatters it.

خودی کو کر بلند اتنا کہ ہر تقدیر سے پہلے
خدا بندے سے خود پوچھے، بتا تیری رضا کیا ہے

Khudi ko kar buland itna ke har taqdeer se pehle
Khuda bande se khud poochhe, bata teri raza kya hai

Raise your selfhood so high that, before each decree,
God Himself asks you: tell me, what is it you wish?

Muhammad Iqbal · Bal-e-Jibril

Read it as theology and it is audacious. Read it as a professional standard and it is simply the job: raise the quality of your own judgment to the point where it has to be consulted — not overwritten, not assumed, not quietly routed around. That is what I am trying to build, and what I am trying to measure.

New York career, FIU roots.

I built a career in New York — Citi, JPMorgan Chase, and before that a regulator's seat — and I did it while keeping one foot firmly at FIU. The doctorate isn't a detour from that work; it's the part that lets me ask the questions the day job doesn't have time for.

What I want next is a teaching arm. Two decades of regulatory reality — consent orders, exam findings, the gap between a control on paper and a control that holds — is worth more in a classroom than another deck of frameworks. I'm working toward teaching and guest lecturing in risk, controls and AI governance, and building the research record to earn that seat.

And I'd rather do the research with people than alone. My own study ran into a wall that a bigger network would have seen sooner: specialist populations are extraordinarily hard to reach. Association partnerships, firm access, cohort collaboration — that is a genuine two-way trade. Organizations get findings about their own people; the research gets its sample.
